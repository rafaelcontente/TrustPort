# The Story Behind TrustPort

## The Problem That Would Not Leave Me Alone

In 2023, a major European bank deployed an LLM to triage credit applications. The model was impressive: it read income statements, parsed employment history, and wrote approval memos that sounded like they came from a senior loan officer. The board was thrilled. The CTO was relieved. The compliance team was quiet.

Six months later, an internal audit found that the model had been approving loans to applicants with negative income. Fluently. Confidently. With beautiful memos explaining why the applicant was creditworthy. The model had learned that "approve" was the most common outcome in its training data, and it had generalized that pattern beyond the boundaries of arithmetic.

Nobody had built a gate.

This is not a story about one bank. It is the story of an industry rushing to put LLMs in front of decisions that have legal, financial, and human consequences — without building the deterministic layer that says "this output cannot leave the building until it passes these rules."

The industry's answer has been to build bigger models, better prompts, more sophisticated RLHF. These are real advances. They do not solve the problem. A model that is 99% accurate on credit decisions is still wrong 1% of the time. In a portfolio of 100,000 loans, that is 1,000 decisions that should not have been made. The cost is not abstract. It is capital. It is regulatory action. It is a customer whose life is disrupted by a decision that no human would have made.

## The Gap in the Market

Every AI vendor is selling intelligence. Nobody is selling restraint.

The market is full of:
- **LLM orchestration frameworks** (LangChain, LlamaIndex) that make it easier to chain models together.
- **Evaluation platforms** (Arize, Weights & Biases) that measure model performance after the fact.
- **Guardrails libraries** (NeMo Guardrails, Guardrails AI) that filter inputs and outputs with regex and classifiers.

What is missing is a **mandatory validation layer** that sits between the model and the real world, with the following properties:
1. **Deterministic**: the same input always produces the same verdict. No sampling, no temperature, no "it depends on the prompt."
2. **Explainable by construction**: every refusal carries a reason code and an actionable counterfactual. Not a post-hoc explanation generated by another model, but a trace of which rule was violated.
3. **Formally verifiable**: the constraint system is not a list of `if` statements that drift over time, but a solver that reasons about the interaction of constraints as a whole.
4. **Audit-ready**: every decision is logged with a hash chain, a policy version, and a timestamp. A compliance officer can reconstruct the state of the system at any point in time.
5. **Fail-closed**: when the validator itself fails (solver timeout, missing policy, internal error), the default is REFUSED, never APPROVED.

This is not a product category that exists yet. It is a product category that should exist, because the alternative is to trust the model, and trust is not a control.

## The Vision

The vision is simple: **every AI-mediated decision that has a real-world consequence passes through a deterministic port before it reaches a human or a system of record.**

This is not about replacing LLMs. It is about complementing them with the one thing they cannot provide: a guarantee. The model proposes. The port decides. The human reviews only the cases that the port cannot resolve.

In credit, this means:
- The LLM reads the application, extracts the features, and proposes an approval or refusal.
- The port checks the proposal against schema, policy, formal constraints, uncertainty bounds, and explanation fidelity.
- If the proposal passes all five gates, it is approved. If it fails, it is repaired, escalated, or refused with a counterfactual the customer can act on.
- Every decision is logged in an append-only audit store with a hash chain that makes tampering detectable.

In healthcare, this means:
- The LLM suggests a diagnosis or treatment plan.
- The port checks it against clinical guidelines, drug interaction databases, and patient history.
- If the suggestion is outside the bounds of safe practice, it is escalated to a human.

In legal, this means:
- The LLM drafts a contract clause or legal argument.
- The port checks it against jurisdiction-specific rules, precedent, and ethical guidelines.
- If the draft violates a constraint, it is refused with a citation to the rule that was broken.

The port is domain-agnostic. The constraints are domain-specific. The architecture is the same.

## The Result We Are Building Toward

The immediate result is a reference implementation of the port for credit decisions, with:
- Five serial gates (schema, rules, Z3 formal verification, conformal uncertainty, fidelity).
- A SQLite audit store with hash chain integrity.
- A FastAPI HTTP wrapper with automatic OpenAPI spec.
- 71 automated tests, including property-based testing with Hypothesis.
- 84% code coverage, trancada no CI.
- A design philosophy document that explains every choice and every rejected alternative.

The long-term result is a **standard for AI validation** that can be adopted by any organization that uses LLMs for decisions with real-world consequences. The standard would define:
- The minimum set of gates that every validation layer must implement.
- The audit trail that every decision must produce.
- The fail-closed behavior that every validator must exhibit.
- The testing methodology that every validator must pass.

This is not a moonshot. It is a necessary piece of infrastructure that the industry will build, with or without us. The question is whether it will be built by the people who understand the problem (the ones who have seen the model approve a loan with negative income) or by the people who are selling the next big LLM.

## Why This Project Exists

This project exists because I believe that the most important thing we can build right now is not a smarter model, but a safer one. The models will get smarter on their own. The safety layer will not build itself.

The project is open-source because the standard should not be owned by a single company. It is MIT-licensed because the goal is adoption, not revenue. It is documented with the honesty of a post-mortem because the only way to build trust is to show the work, including the mistakes.

The project is called TrustPort because a port is a place where things are checked before they enter or leave. A port is not the destination. It is the gate. And in a world where AI is making decisions that affect people's lives, the gate is the most important thing we can build.

---

If you are building an AI system that makes decisions with real-world consequences, and you do not have a deterministic validation layer between the model and the real world, you are not building a system. You are building a liability.

TrustPort is the start of the answer. It is not the end. The end is a world where every AI-mediated decision is validated, auditable, and explainable by construction. That world is possible. It is just not the one we are building by default.

This project is an attempt to change the default.
